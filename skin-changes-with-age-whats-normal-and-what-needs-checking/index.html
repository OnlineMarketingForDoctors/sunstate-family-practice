Skin Changes With Age: What’s Normal and What Needs Checking

Everyone’s skin changes gradually over time, so some changes are easy to miss.

A new spot on your arm, a mole that looks darker than usual, or skin that feels thinner than last year may seem minor, but noticing these differences early can affect how quickly it can be assessed and managed if needed.

Many changes are harmless, but some are worth checking.

What’s in this article

  • How skin naturally changes with age
  • Normal skin changes to expect
  • Red flags to watch for
  • Comparing moles and lesions
  • How Total Body Mapping (TBM) can help

Skin is our largest organ and is constantly renewing itself, so over time, natural changes occur.

Skin becomes thinner, less elastic, the tone may vary, and healing can be slower.

These shifts happen due to a combination of genetics, hormones, and environmental factors such as sun exposure.

For example, someone who spends a lot of time on the beach may notice small, flat patches on their hands or shoulders from accumulated sun exposure.

Others may see subtle sagging around the jawline or fine lines around the eyes.

Most age‑related spots are benign, but new or changing lesions should be assessed if you are unsure.

Normal skin changes
Some changes are part of the natural ageing process and usually harmless. Here are some common examples:

Fine lines and wrinkles:Appear where skin stretches most, such as around the eyes and mouth, caused by gradual collagen loss.

Dryness and rough texture:Less oil production leads to slightly rougher skin, especially on arms and legs.

Age spots:Small, flat patches from sun exposure over the years, commonly seen on hands, shoulders, and face.

Mild sagging:Loss of firmness around the jawline or upper arms is common.

Slower healing:Minor scratches or cuts take longer to repair.
These changes are generally predictable and do not require urgent medical attention, but noticing them helps you track what is normal for your skin.

Red flags to watch
Some changes should be checked sooner rather than later. These include:

New or changing moles:Look for spots that grow quickly, change shape, develop uneven colour, or have irregular borders. Even small changes over a few weeks to months can be significant.

Use the ABCDE + ‘ugly duckling’ method:Doctors often use the ABCDE rule (Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter, Evolving) and the ‘ugly duckling’ sign — a spot that looks different from your others.

Persistent sores or itchy spots:Any sore, scab, or lesion that does not heal within two to three weeks should be assessed.

Lumps, thickened patches, or crusty areas:These can sometimes represent skin cancers or other conditions that need assessment.

Sudden pigmentation changes:Dark patches, redness around a mole, or new streaks of colour can indicate a problem. Even changes in freckles or areas of long-standing sun damage should be checked.

Add nodular melanoma caution:Some fast‑growing melanomas may appear as a firm, raised, evenly coloured lump — any rapidly growing new lesion should be checked.

If you notice any of these signs, consider booking a skin check with your GP.

The GPs at Sunstate Family Practice may recommend monitoring, further investigation, or procedures depending on the findings.

If something needs treatment, earlier assessment can sometimes mean simpler treatment options.

Normal moles are usually symmetrical, even in col our, and stay fairly consistent over time. Suspicious moles may:

  • Change in size, shape, or colour over weeks or months
  • Develop uneven edges or multiple shades
  • Appear raised or start bleeding or itching

If you see any of these changes, even if subtle, it is time to book a skin check.

“Did that always look like that?” It’s a question many people ask themselves.

A good idea is to make a note in your phone, ideally with a few photos, and compare changes over time. Keep images private and bring them to your GP rather than sharing online.

If you notice anything suspicious, or aren’t sure, a simple skin check can confirm whether action needs to be taken, and offer peace of mind.

Sunstate Family Practice offers comprehensive skin checks and optional Total Body Mapping for higher‑risk patients.

Total Body Mapping (TBM)helps create a visual record of your skin over time. Using precise imaging, TBM captures moles, freckles, and lesions from consistent angles and lighting, making it easier to compare changes between visits.

TBM is particularly helpful if you have a large number of moles, fair skin, a history of sun exposure, or a personal or family history of melanoma.

Your GP can advise whether TBM is appropriate for your risk level.

It can help document areas that are hard to see yourself, such as the back, shoulders, and scalp.

After your appointment, your GP will discuss what to watch for and when review may be recommended.

TBM is an adjunct to, not a replacement for, a clinical skin examination.

Learn more about Total Body Mapping at Sunstate Family Practicehere, or take a look at theCancer Council’s skin check guidance.

If you spot a new growth, changing mole, or persistent sore, book a skin check with your GP.

At Sunstate Family Practice, the GPs provide practical guidance, personalised monitoring, and clear explanations of findings and next steps.

Remember that a skin check, including TBM, is non-invasive, typically painless and performed privately.
